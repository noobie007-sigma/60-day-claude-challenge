DECISION REPORT

Landing a full-time offer before your contract ends — how to structure your daily grind

July 15, 2026

The Real Decision

It's not "should I study" — it's how much of your body and mind you're willing to spend to do it. The real trade-off is speed of prep vs. sustainability of effort over 5+ months. It's hard because "go all out" feels like the only way to make up for a weak DSA base and zero System Design knowledge, but you already suspect it could break you before December.

The Case For Each Option

Option A — All-Out Grind (sacrifice sleep, squeeze every hour)

Strongest case: Maximum raw study hours squeezed out of a brutal schedule; feels like it directly attacks the "not enough time" problem.

Hidden upside: Short bursts of high intensity can build momentum fast if it doesn't collapse — early wins on easy DSA topics could boost confidence quickly.

Biggest weakness: Sleep deprivation degrades memory consolidation — the exact thing DSA and System Design require. You could study more hours and retain less.

Best if you value: short-term intensity over long-term retention

Option B — Structured, Sustainable Plan (protect sleep, use commute + fixed blocks)

Strongest case: Your commute (4hrs/day) is dead time you can partially reclaim — audio/video DSA content, flashcards, or reading on metro — without touching your sleep.

Hidden upside: Consistency compounds. A tired brain studying 6 hours poorly loses to a rested brain studying 3 hours well, especially over 5 months.

Biggest weakness: Feels slower at first — you won't see dramatic daily progress, which can feel demoralizing given the pressure you're under.

Best if you value: durability and actually reaching December intact
Assumption Buster
  • You're assuming more hours = more preparation. Retention, not hours, is what converts to interview performance.
  • You're assuming health is something you can "pay back" later. Sleep debt and burnout don't reverse on a schedule — they show up as brain fog exactly when you need to perform in interviews.
  • You're assuming your commute time is unusable. It's 4 hours/day you haven't accounted for as study time yet — that alone could be your DSA-practice block.

Biases at play:

Sunk Cost / Effort Justification All-or-Nothing Thinking

What you're definitely ignoring: Interview performance requires a sharp, rested mind on the day — not just accumulated knowledge. A burned-out fresher who "knew everything" but blanks in the interview still gets rejected.

Premortem

Option A — All-Out Grind: it's 12 months later and it failed

  • Burnout by month 2-3 caused you to skip study entirely for stretches, netting less total prep than a steady pace would have.
  • Sleep-deprived brain underperformed in actual interviews despite "knowing" the material.
  • Physical health issues (common with chronic sleep loss) forced downtime you didn't plan for.

Early warning sign: You start dozing off at work or forgetting things you studied the night before.

Prevention: Set a hard sleep floor (e.g. 6 hrs minimum) as non-negotiable, no matter how behind you feel.

Option B — Structured Plan: it's 12 months later and it failed

  • The plan felt too slow and you lost urgency, letting weeks slip without real progress.
  • Commute-based study didn't stick because metro conditions (crowds, noise) made focus hard.
  • You under-invested in System Design because it felt less urgent than DSA, then got caught off guard in interviews.

Early warning sign: A week goes by where you can't say what specific topic you covered.

Prevention: Weekly checklist with named topics, reviewed every Sunday — no vague "I studied" allowed.

7-Day Test Plan
Day 1-2
Research: list DSA topics by frequency, and find one structured System Design resource (course/playlist) to commit to.
Day 3-4
Experiment: try studying DSA on the metro commute using a phone/notes app — measure how much you actually retain.
Day 5-6
Conversation: talk to a senior/mentor in your field about realistic prep timelines for DSA + System Design as a fresher.
Day 7
Decision day: if commute-study retention felt usable, commit to Option B fully; if it felt useless, redesign around weekend-heavy blocks instead of sleep sacrifice.
The Verdict
Option B: Structured, Sustainable Plan

It wins because your biggest fear — "getting nothing after sacrificing my health" — is a real risk under Option A and largely avoided under Option B. Reclaiming your 4-hour commute as study time gets you real hours without touching sleep, which protects the very thing (retention, interview-day sharpness) that actually gets you hired.

What could flip it: if you test the commute-study approach and find you truly retain nothing on the metro — then a revised plan with more weekend intensity (not sleep sacrifice) is the next best move.

The hard truth: you don't have a time problem, you have an allocation problem — and burning your health won't fix an allocation problem.
